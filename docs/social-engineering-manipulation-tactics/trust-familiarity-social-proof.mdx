# Trust, Familiarity & Social Proof

Trust, Familiarity & Social Proof describes how ordinary human instincts can be turned into pressure, access, or compliance. The tactic works by making a request feel familiar, urgent, rewarding, or emotionally difficult to question.

These attacks are social before they are technical. Recognising the pattern creates time to pause, verify the person and request independently, and choose a safer response.

## Myth Busters

**A familiar name is a verified identity.**  
Repeated exposure makes someone feel safe without proving who they are.

**Mutual connections make a request trustworthy.**  
Connections, endorsements, and profiles can be copied or manufactured.

**Many positive reviews prove a service is legitimate.**  
Reviews can be purchased, coordinated, or generated.

**If colleagues already complied, the request is safe.**  
Social proof creates pressure but does not replace independent verification.

## The Story

**The Setup**  

Riya, a new finance executive, accepted a LinkedIn request from “Karan Mehta,” who used her company logo, a senior title, and mutual connections including her manager.





For two weeks, Karan offered helpful process advice and appeared regularly in her feed. Then he asked her to process a vendor payment, claiming a system migration had delayed the purchase order.

Riya had never met him, but his name felt familiar and his social proof looked convincing. She processed the payment. The profile, connections, and helpful behaviour had all been manufactured during her onboarding.

**The Mere Exposure Effect**  

The more you see someone, the more positively you evaluate them - even if nothing about them has changed.

This is why warm-up attacks work on experienced professionals. The brain's trust mechanism doesn't verify whether familiarity was earned or faked.

**Liking & Similarity**  

People comply more readily with requests from someone they find likeable or similar to themselves - shared hometown, shared alma mater, shared hobby, even a shared complaint about the weather.

**Unity - "One of Us" Framing**  

A step beyond similarity: unity works by framing the target and the attacker as sharing an identity, not just a preference.

**Fake Reviews & Social Proof**  

When you can't independently evaluate something, you trust what others say. The problem: social proof is trivially easy to fake.

Scam platforms display fake profit screenshots. Fake recruitment agencies post testimonials from people who don't exist. Fraud pages use AI-generated endorsements.

Before trusting reviews on an unfamiliar platform:
- Search the company name + "scam" or "complaint" on independent sites
- Verify registration with the regulatory body directly
- Check for reviews on platforms the company doesn't control

## What the Experience Shows

**Familiarity is a feeling, not verification.**  
Your brain treats "I've seen this before" as safety. Attackers manufacture that feeling. It tells you nothing about who they actually are.

**Fake reviews influence you even when you know they exist.**  
Awareness helps only if you actively verify independently. Passive knowledge isn't enough.

**Context is the weapon.**  
Karan didn't claim to be CEO. He claimed to be an ordinary colleague with the right internal details. That context is what made the request feel legitimate.

**The ask comes after the trust.**  
A stranger's request triggers skepticism. The same request from someone familiar triggers compliance. The warm-up period is the entire attack.
## Take Control: What Can You Do?

:::info[Familiarity is not verification]

A known name, shared contact, or convincing profile can be copied. Confirm unexpected requests using a separate channel.

:::


**Verify requests through a separate channel.**  
Someone asks for money, access, or data via LinkedIn? Call your HR department or a known colleague. The message is not proof of identity.

**Search for independent reviews before trusting them.**  
On unfamiliar platforms, search the company name on Google and independent review sites. Only positive reviews on their own site is a red flag.

**Question "everyone else did it."**  
This is pressure, not information. Verify with the specific people mentioned - not with the person making the claim.

**Verify new connections before complying with requests.**  
Check their employment through your internal directory or a colleague - not through a link they provide.
