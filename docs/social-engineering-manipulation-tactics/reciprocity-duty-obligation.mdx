# Reciprocity, Duty & Obligation

Reciprocity, Duty & Obligation describes how ordinary human instincts can be turned into pressure, access, or compliance. The tactic works by making a request feel familiar, urgent, rewarding, or emotionally difficult to question.

These attacks are social before they are technical. Recognising the pattern creates time to pause, verify the person and request independently, and choose a safer response.

## Myth Busters

**A free gift creates a real obligation.**  
Unsolicited reports, praise, or help can be bait for a later request.

**Refusing a large request means accepting the smaller one is fair.**  
The large request may be designed only to make the payload feel reasonable.

**Helping someone at work means skipping policy.**  
Attackers impersonate roles that make compliance feel like a job duty.

**Empathy is proof that the person needs help.**  
Distress can be staged to bypass the checks you would normally use.

## The Story

**The Premium Report**  

Liam, a junior financial analyst, received an unsolicited industry report from “Marcus,” a senior partner at a respected consulting firm. Marcus praised Liam’s work and offered the expensive report for free.

A few days later, Marcus asked Liam to open a “candidate information” PDF for a hiring project. Liam hesitated, so Marcus first requested a much larger favour, then returned with the smaller PDF request as a compromise. Feeling indebted and eager to help, Liam opened it.

The report was bait. The large request was designed to be rejected, and the smaller request carried the malicious payload.

**Unsolicited Favours and the Obligation They Create**  

When someone gives us something - even if we didn't ask for it - we feel a subconscious pressure to return the favour.

Free software, premium reports, insider tips, and warm professional praise are not generous. They are investments in your compliance.

True generosity has no strings attached. In the digital realm, unexpected gifts from strangers almost always do.

**Concession Tactics and the Empathy Gap**  

Also known as the "door-in-the-face" technique: ask for something unreasonable first. When refused, immediately offer something much smaller.

Because they "compromised," you feel pressure to compromise in return.

Attackers also weaponise empathy directly. They impersonate confused new hires, stressed vendors, or elderly customers.

**Duty, Workplace Roles, and Recognising the Setup**  

HR staff open attachments. Customer support responds to external queries. IT resolves tickets from unknown users. These are job requirements - and attackers know every one of them.

Impersonating executives, legal entities, or IT departments specifically targets role-based duty.

Generosity as a setup has one tell: the ask follows the gift.

## What the Experience Shows

**Rejection is part of the plan.**  
When you say no to the first request, you feel like you won. Attackers design the first request to be rejected. Your relief and guilt make the follow-up request significantly easier to accept.

**Generosity is an offensive weapon.**  
Free reports, unprompted help, and glowing compliments are psychological levers. The gift installs an invisible debt in your mind that you will subconsciously try to repay.

**Empathy overrides policy.**  
When a "vendor" is panicking or a "new hire" is confused, your instinct is to help. Attackers engineer that distress to bypass the security checks you would otherwise apply.

**The warmth is the preparation.**  
By the time the ask comes, weeks of rapport have already done the work. The gift and the relationship are not context - they are the attack.
## Take Control: What Can You Do?

**Beware the follow-up ask.**  
If someone makes a large unusual request that you refuse, treat the immediate "smaller" favour that follows as a red flag, not a compromise.

**Neutralise unsolicited favours.**  
If a stranger provides a valuable asset out of nowhere, do not engage. Classify unsolicited digital generosity as a potential setup, not kindness.

**Pause when empathy surges.**  
When you feel a sudden urge to help a confused coworker or a stressed vendor, take 60 seconds. Ask yourself: "Am I about to violate a security policy because I feel bad for this person?"
